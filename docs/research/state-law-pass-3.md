## Part 2 — The IRA Part D Mechanism, Precisely

The claim that the IRA's Medicare Part D redesign ($2,000 OOP cap in 2025,
rising to $2,100 in 2026) is causing manufacturer commercial copay-card
maximums to reset from roughly $15,000–20,000 down to $2,000–3,000 is
overstated or incorrect as stated. Manufacturer commercial copay/savings
cards already exclude Medicare beneficiaries by design (and by
anti-kickback rules); they require commercial insurance.

### Actual IRA Part D redesign elements (primary CMS sources)
- **2025**: $2,000 annual beneficiary OOP threshold; coverage-gap phase
  eliminated; new Manufacturer Discount Program (replacing Coverage Gap
  Discount Program) with manufacturer liability typically 10% in initial
  coverage and 20% in catastrophic for applicable drugs.
- **2026**: OOP threshold adjusted to $2,100; continued Manufacturer
  Discount Program and selected-drug subsidy under negotiation.
  Beneficiaries pay 0% once the threshold is reached. Medicare
  Prescription Payment Plan (M3P) allows spreading OOP costs into monthly
  installments.

### Causal chain for the claim
Commercial copay cards themselves are not being reset by the Part D OOP
cap; they remain commercial-only.

Possible related pressures (not a direct "card maximum resets to $2k–3k"
mechanism):
- Manufacturer liability under the new Discount Program and Drug Price
  Negotiation Program increases manufacturer exposure on the Medicare
  side, which can affect overall patient-support budgets and willingness
  to fund high commercial-card maximums or foundation grants.
- Independent charity foundations (which can assist Medicare patients
  under strict anti-kickback safe-harbor rules) may adjust grant designs
  or availability in response to the lower Medicare OOP burden and
  manufacturer funding changes.
- Some manufacturers have reduced or restructured commercial-card terms
  for other reasons (maximizer detection language, annual caps already in
  the low-to-mid thousands for many products, or specific negotiated
  drugs). Example language in current cards shows monthly/annual maxima
  that can be reduced further if a maximizer is detected, but this is not
  driven by the $2,000 Part D figure.

No primary CMS, manufacturer, or regulatory document reviewed establishes
a direct causal reset of commercial copay-card annual maxima to the Part D
OOP number. The claim appears to conflate Medicare beneficiary OOP relief,
manufacturer Medicare discount liability, and separate commercial-card or
foundation design choices. If the claim refers only to foundation/PAP
behavior for Medicare patients, that is more plausible but still requires
case-by-case manufacturer or foundation documentation rather than a
uniform $2k–3k reset.

## Part 3 — Accumulator/Maximizer Prevalence by State and Payer

### National prevalence (commercial)
- Plans offering the design: ~80–84% of commercial lives are in plans that
  have accumulators available in design; ~73–81% for maximizers
  (MMIT/Drug Channels 2024–2025 data).
- Actual enrollment in active programs: lower — approximately 39–43% of
  commercial covered lives in accumulator plans and ~39–47% in maximizer
  plans in recent years (MMIT, Drug Channels, IQVIA analyses). KFF 2024
  Employer Health Benefits Survey: 17% of large firms (500+) report a
  copay accumulator in their largest plan (rising to 34% for firms with
  5,000+ workers); self-funded plans higher than fully insured.

### By state
No comprehensive public ranking or map of actual prevalence (share of
lives) by state was located. State bans reduce prevalence in the fully
insured/marketplace segment of those states (Avalere: state bans affect
~17% of total U.S. commercial lives). Specialty-drug patient-level
prevalence (IQVIA) varies by therapeutic area and is higher for
oncology/autoimmune than average. One IQVIA-derived state view of combined
accumulator/maximizer prevalence among select specialty users showed wide
variation (examples in secondary materials ranged roughly 15–43% by state
in 2023 data).

### Common vendor-administered programs
- SaveOnSP (associated with Express Scripts/Cigna; often requires Accredo
  specialty pharmacy).
- PrudentRx (associated with CVS Caremark).

These are repeatedly cited as the major maximizer/optimization vehicles.
No independent published "market-share ranking" with exact percentages
was found beyond qualitative dominance of the two large PBM-linked
programs.

### Source bias note
PhRMA/patient-advocacy sources (AIDS Institute, NORD, All Copays Count
coalitions) emphasize patient harm and higher effective prevalence.
PBM/insurer-adjacent or consulting sources (Drug Channels, MMIT, Avalere,
IQVIA) frame the programs as cost-containment tools and report both
design availability and actual uptake. KFF is closer to neutral survey
data. Numbers differ by methodology (plan design availability vs. lives
actually subject to the program vs. specialty-patient experience).

## Part 4 — Real-World Complaint Patterns (for building test cases)

Patterns drawn from documented complaints (BBB, patient-advocacy stories,
Reddit-style forums via secondary reports, X posts, consumer articles).
Generalized shapes only; no personal identifiers.

### 1. Coupon/copay card rejected or not honored at the pharmacy counter despite advertised acceptance
**Pattern**: Patient presents manufacturer savings card or third-party
coupon (GoodRx, etc.); pharmacy system rejects it, states "too low,"
technical glitch, or "not accepted here." Patient is charged full cash or
insurance price.

**Sources**: BBB complaints against discount-card platforms (GoodRx
examples of quoted price not matching at counter, 2025–2026); X posts
(e.g., GoodRx/Walgreens "too low" rejection, early 2026; other coupon
denials); consumer articles on independent pharmacies refusing cards that
produce negative reimbursements.

### 2. Online/quoted price does not match the price actually charged
**Pattern**: Website or app shows a low cash or coupon price; at the
counter the price is substantially higher, membership is required, or the
coupon has expired/updated silently.

**Sources**: BBB GoodRx and telehealth-platform complaints (2025–2026); X
posts describing quoted vs. actual pharmacy price mismatches.

### 3. Accumulator or maximizer unexpectedly nullifies expected savings ("surprise cliff")
**Pattern**: Patient uses manufacturer assistance for months, believing it
advances deductible/OOP; later discovers payments never counted. Mid-year
bill arrives for the full deductible or remaining OOP once the card is
exhausted. Plan language may have been opaque ("specialty copay offset,"
"out-of-pocket protection," etc.).

**Sources**: Patient-advocacy first-person accounts (Patients Rising,
2023–2026 stories); Hemophilia Federation and Accessia Health educational
posts describing mid-year cost shocks; general X/advocacy commentary on
accumulator surprise.

### 4. Foundation/charity fund closes or becomes unavailable shortly after enrollment/application
**Pattern**: Patient is approved or in process for independent foundation
assistance; fund then closes, pauses, or exhausts, leaving the patient
without coverage and facing high costs. (Less frequent in the sampled
recent X/BBB results than the three patterns above, but repeatedly noted
in specialty-disease advocacy literature.)

**Sources**: Advocacy materials and secondary patient reports (PAN
Foundation–style discussions and rare-disease forums); limited direct
recent X hits in the search window.

These patterns are suitable for test-case construction: (a) card
acceptance failure at POS, (b) quote-vs-actual price delta, (c) assistance
applied but not credited to deductible/OOP, (d) external fund status
change after enrollment. All remain unverified anecdotes or aggregated
complaints until primary documentation is pulled for any specific tool.
